# The Operator Method

**A non-linear way to refactor a government without pretending you can start from a blank page.**

Most policy work is linear: identify a problem, design a program, fund it, deliver it, evaluate it. That works for simple problems. It fails for a government because the real problem is usually the **system around the program**: duplicated agencies, misaligned budgets, perverse incentives, workforce bottlenecks, Commonwealth conditions, council boundaries, community trust and the election cycle.

This method treats government as an operating system. You do not rewrite the whole thing at once. You find the interfaces, remove the duplication, standardise the plumbing, diversify the front line, and sequence the changes so that trust survives the transition.

---

## 1. Start with the citizen, not the portfolio

Write the citizen promise in one sentence, in plain language, with a number and a date if possible.

- Weak: "Improve health outcomes."
- Strong: "When you call an ambulance, you will be handed over to an emergency department within 40 minutes — 90% of the time — by 2029."

Then map the **actual journey** the citizen takes, including every door they have to find, every form they have to repeat, every wait they endure and every dollar they pay. The journey is usually cross-portfolio. That is the point.

---

## 2. Use six lenses, in parallel

Adapted from Edward de Bono's six thinking hats. The lenses are not stages; they are perspectives that should be run in parallel and then reconciled.

### White — evidence

- What do we know? What is the source, the date and the confidence?
- What is measured, what is assumed, and what is missing?
- What would change our mind?
- Who holds the data we do not have?

### Red — trust and lived experience

- How does this feel to the citizen, the frontline worker, the family, the community?
- Where is the distrust coming from: past failure, process, language, or power?
- Who is not in the room?
- What would make a frontline worker recommend this to a colleague?

### Black — risk, law and accountability

- What can go wrong: fiscal, operational, legal, safety, reputational?
- Who is accountable when it fails?
- What does the statute, the enterprise agreement, the Commonwealth agreement or the treaty require?
- What is the pre-mortem: it is 2030, the policy failed — why?

### Yellow — value

- What is the economic, social, fiscal and environmental return?
- Who captures the value, and who bears the cost?
- What is the avoided cost, and who books it?
- What is the cost of doing nothing?

### Green — reframe

- What if the problem were defined differently?
- What if the default were reversed: public instead of private, prevention instead of response, shared instead of owned, rules instead of appeals, outcomes instead of inputs?
- What would we do with the same money but a different structure?
- What would a bold, competent operator do in the first 100 days?

### Blue — sequence and system

- What must happen first?
- What can run in parallel?
- Who owns the whole system, not just a slice?
- What is the decision gate, the evidence gate and the exit gate?
- How do we protect the reform from the next election cycle?

## 3. Apply the provocation cards

Use these to break the linear frame. Not every card fits every portfolio; the point is to force at least three uncomfortable alternatives before settling on a design.

1. **Public default.** What would it look like if the state owned or operated the core capability?
2. **Citizen budget.** What if we added up every cost a household pays to government and designed around that total?
3. **Outcome purchase.** What if we bought the result and let providers organise the inputs?
4. **Reverse tender.** Here is the outcome and the price. Who can deliver it?
5. **Shared asset.** What if the asset were shared across portfolios, place or time?
6. **One front door.** What if the citizen never had to know which agency owns the problem?
7. **Merge the back, separate the front.** What if corporate services were consolidated while frontline teams were empowered?
8. **Regulator-operator split.** Is the state too close to the thing it regulates, or too far from the thing it operates?
9. **Prevention ledger.** What if avoided costs were booked to the budget that funded the prevention?
10. **Price the externality.** What if the wasteful choice carried its true cost?
11. **Sunset everything.** What if every entity, board and program had to reapply for its existence every five years?
12. **Place deal.** What if the state negotiated one integrated deal per region instead of 20 separate grants?
13. **Common platform.** What if the data, identity, payments, rostering or booking layer were built once?
14. **Workforce as infrastructure.** What if the pipeline of nurses, teachers, carers, drivers and builders were planned like a road?
15. **Procurement as industrial policy.** What if the state's purchasing power were used deliberately to build local capability?
16. **Mutualise it.** What if the users, workers or community owned the service?
17. **Insure it.** What if the state insured the risk instead of delivering the response?
18. **Stop it.** What if the best policy were to stop doing something, and return the money?
19. **Federal deal.** What if we renegotiated the Commonwealth conditions instead of absorbing them?
20. **Trust sequence.** What if we did the visible thing first, banked the trust, and used it to fund the structural thing?

## 4. Keep four ledgers

Every serious policy moves value between four ledgers. If you only track the fiscal one, you will miss the reason the policy fails.

| Ledger | Question | Example metric |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiscal** | What does it cost, what does it save, and when? | Net operating balance, cost per outcome |
| **Capability** | Can the public service actually deliver it? | Workforce pipeline, skills, systems, contract management |
| **Trust** | Will citizens and frontline staff believe it? | Service experience, complaint resolution, confidence |
| **Time** | How long until the citizen feels it? | Days to first visible outcome, years to structural reform |

A policy that saves money but destroys capability is a false economy. A policy that builds trust but never changes the fiscal trajectory is a performance. The operator's job is to trade across all four.

---

## 5. Choose the delivery model explicitly

For every service, choose one of six models and state why:

| Model | Use when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Make** | The capability is core, strategic or natural monopoly | Cost creep, low innovation, workforce rigidity |
| **Buy** | The market is competitive and outcomes are contractible | Thin markets, lock-in, contractor mark-ups |
| **Share** | Common infrastructure or back office | Governance, cost allocation, free-riding |
| **Regulate** | The market can deliver but needs rules | Capture, complexity, enforcement capacity |
| **Insure** | The risk is rare but catastrophic | Moral hazard, uninsurable risk, slow payout |
| **Enable** | Community, local government or social enterprise is closer to the problem | Fragility, inequity, accountability gaps |

The default in Victoria has drifted toward **buy** for services and **make** for bureaucracy. The reframe is often the reverse: **make** the core capability and **buy** the commodity; **share** the back office and **enable** the community.

---

## 6. Map the ecosystem and negotiate the deal

Government does not act on a market; it acts in one. For each policy area, identify:

- **Funders:** Treasury, Commonwealth, councils, philanthropists, investors.
- **Deliverers:** departments, agencies, councils, contractors, social enterprises, community organisations, unions, universities, TAFEs.
- **Rule-makers:** Parliament, regulators, courts, Commonwealth, treaty bodies.
- **Beneficiaries and losers:** who gains, who pays, who objects.
- **Data holders:** who knows whether it worked.
- **Gatekeepers:** planning, procurement, industrial relations, cabinet, media.

Then write the **deal**: what each party gives, what each party gets, and what happens if they do not move. A policy without a deal is a wish.

---

## 7. Use the cut/add ledger

For every proposal, complete the ledger:

| Cut | Add | Offset / return | Who objects | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Rules:

- No new money without a named offset, a sunset or a measured return.
- Cut the **activity**, not necessarily the **people**: redeploy, retrain, transfer.
- Cut the **duplication**, not the **front line**.
- Add the **capability**, not just the **program**.
- If you cannot name who objects, you have not understood the system.

---

## 8. Run the policy as a product lifecycle

| Stage | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Discover** | What is the real problem and who feels it? | Citizen journey, evidence note |
| **Frame** | What are the three viable models? | Options paper, six-lens scan |
| **Prototype** | What can we test small and fast? | Pilot, sandbox, procurement probe |
| **Prove** | What does the evidence say? | Evaluation, cost-benefit, red team |
| **Scale** | What has to be true to go statewide? | Business case, delivery plan |
| **Embed** | Who owns it, and how does it stay accountable? | Legislation, funding, performance regime |
| **Retire** | When and how will we stop it? | Sunset clause, transition plan |

Most government policies skip **prototype** and **retire**. Those are the two stages that protect the public purse.

---

## 9. Red-team before you announce

Ask four people to attack the policy:

1. **The sceptical Treasurer:** "Show me the offset, the risk and the operating cost in year four."
2. **The frontline worker:** "This will fail on a Tuesday afternoon because..."
3. **The council or community partner:** "You have not funded the thing you are asking us to do."
4. **The citizen who has been failed before:** "Why should I believe this time?"

If the policy survives those four, it is ready for the public. If it does not, fix it before the announcement, not after.

---

## 9b. Pass the democracy test

A policy can be economically sound and still be democratically illegitimate. Before it goes public, answer five questions in public:

1. **Who decided?** Name the minister, the public servant and the statutory decision-maker.
2. **On what evidence?** Publish the baseline, the source, the assumptions and the options.
3. **Who influenced them?** Disclose meetings, lobbying, donations, advice and conflicts.
4. **What did it cost, and who pays?** Show the whole-of-life cost, the offset and the distribution.
5. **How can a citizen see the record and challenge it?** Publish the decision record, the review date and the appeal or complaint path.

If the answer is "you can't", the policy is not ready. The full reform agenda is in [`../02-levers/democracy-transparency-and-operator-culture/`](../02-levers/democracy-transparency-and-operator-culture/README.md).

## 10. Sequence for trust

A reform program has three clocks:

- **The 100-day clock:** visible, low-risk, high-signal changes that prove the government can do what it said.
- **The 2-year clock:** structural changes that require legislation, procurement, workforce transition and Commonwealth agreement.
- **The 10-year clock:** the big shifts in housing, energy, health, education and the federation.

Do not announce the 10-year clock as if it will deliver in year one. Do not let the 100-day clock become the whole strategy. Sequence the three so that each one buys the credibility for the next.

---

## The operator's test

A policy is ready when you can say, in one page:

> Here is the citizen promise. Here is the current system and what it costs. Here is the waste we will stop. Here is the capability we will build. Here is who else must move. Here is the first 100 days. Here is how we will know it worked. Here is when we will stop it if it does not.
