# 02 — Housing and Planning

> **Citizen promise:** A home you can afford, in a place that works.
>
> **Operator thesis:** Victoria cannot approve its way out of the housing crisis while the state owns land, sets the rules and then acts as a passive landlord. The state should become a **master developer and land-value partner**: assemble land, fund infrastructure first, pre-approve designs, and take a share of the value it creates. Planning should be a **rules system**, not an appeals industry.
>
> **2026 baseline:** The state target is **800,000 net new homes between 2024 and 2034** (about 80,000 a year), and **2.24 million additional homes by 2051**. The Victorian Housing Register had **57,604 new applicant households** in September 2026, including **32,947 priority access** households. [A/B]

**Minister(s):** Minister for Housing, Building and Precincts, Homelessness and Seniors (Nick Staikos); Minister for Planning, Attorney-General and Prevention of Family Violence (Sonya Kilkenny).
**Department(s):** Department of Transport and Planning (DTP); Department of Families, Fairness and Housing (DFFH).
**Key agencies:** Homes Victoria; Victorian Planning Authority (VPA); Development Victoria; Building and Plumbing Commission; Victorian Building Authority; local councils; Consumer Affairs Victoria (renters).
**Statute / framework:** Planning and Environment Act; *Planning Amendment (Better Decisions Made Faster) Act 2026*; Housing Statement; Housing Australia Future Fund and National Housing Accord; Local Government Act.
**Scale:** Housing and homelessness spending sits across DFFH and Homes Victoria; planning system covers all 79 councils; social housing waiting list is over 57,000 households. [B]

---

## 1. The outcome

- **Outcome 1:** Enough homes are built in the right places, at prices households can afford.
- **Outcome 2:** Social and affordable housing grows as a permanent asset class, not a crisis response.
- **Outcome 3:** New housing is matched with transport, schools, health, water and community infrastructure.
- **Outcome 4:** Renters have stable, safe and fair homes.
- **Outcome 5:** Planning decisions are fast, predictable and trusted.

**What success looks like by 2030:** Completions consistently above 80,000 a year; the social housing wait falls; new homes are concentrated in established areas with services; infrastructure is funded before residents arrive; planning approvals are measured by time and outcome, not appeal volume.

**What failure looks like:** More targets, more strategies, more permits, but the same affordability crisis and the same waitlist.

## 2. Current machinery

| Layer | Who / what | Role | Spend or scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing | DFFH / Homes Victoria | Social housing, homelessness, rental support | Significant, multi-year |
| Planning | DTP / VPA | Planning system, precincts, growth areas | Whole-of-state |
| Development | Development Victoria | Land development, precincts, partnerships | Project-based |
| Regulation | Building and Plumbing Commission / VBA | Building quality and safety | Whole-of-state |
| Local | 79 councils | Local planning, permits, community infrastructure | Rates and grants |
| Commonwealth | Treasury / Housing Australia | HAFF, National Housing Accord, tax settings | National |

**What the citizen experiences:** A confusing maze of councils, zones, overlays, permits, appeals, lotteries and waitlists. A renter faces rent increases, insecure leases and poor quality. A first-home buyer faces deposit, stamp duty and competition.
**What the frontline experiences:** Council planners drowning in applications; developers gaming appeals; builders managing cost and labour; community housing providers chasing short grants.

---

## 3. Where the money and time leak

- **Approval delay and appeal risk:** Bespoke assessments and third-party appeals add years and cost to projects. The 2026 planning reforms create faster streams, but implementation is staggered into 2027. [A]
- **Infrastructure sequencing:** Growth-area homes are approved before transport, schools, water and community facilities. The state then pays more to retrofit.
- **Fragmented housing agencies:** Homes Victoria, Development Victoria, VPA, DFFH and councils each hold land, powers and funding, but no single entity owns the housing outcome.
- **Underused public land:** Surplus government land is sold for budget receipts instead of used for housing, value capture or mixed-use precincts.
- **Social housing procurement:** One-off projects, complex tenders and short funding cycles raise cost and slow delivery.
- **Rental market failure:** Insecure leases, poor quality and rent stress transfer costs to households and emergency services.
- **Stamp duty:** A transaction tax discourages downsizing and mobility, and narrows the tax base.
- **Building quality:** Defects and insurance failures create avoidable public cost and erode trust.

**Estimated annual value of the leak:** To be costed. The biggest number is not a line item; it is the cost of delay: temporary accommodation, homelessness services, commuting, congestion and lost productivity. [D]

---

## 4. The reframe

**The old frame:** The state sets targets, councils approve, developers build, and government subsidises the gap.

**The new frame:** The state is a **market shaper and master developer**. It uses land, infrastructure, planning rules, finance and procurement to change the economics of housing — and captures part of the value it creates to fund affordable housing.

**Three alternative models:**

| Model | How it works | Upside | Downside | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Target plus subsidy** | Keep targets, boost grants, streamline permits | Familiar; can scale | Does not fix land, infrastructure or delivery capacity | Necessary but not sufficient |
| **B. State master developer** | Land assembly, infrastructure-first precincts, pattern book, value capture, public/private delivery | Changes the economics; captures value | Requires capability, capital and political courage | Preferred base |
| **C. Housing market reset** | Broad land tax, by-right infill, aggressive public building | Fast supply and affordability | Major political and market disruption | Use elements; sequence carefully |

**The reframe that matters most:** **Stop selling public land and start developing it.** A public developer can assemble fragmented sites, fund infrastructure, pre-approve designs, partner with builders and community housing providers, and retain a share of the uplift for affordable homes.

**Supporting moves:**

- **Pattern book and pre-approved designs:** a Victorian pattern book of well-designed, climate-ready homes that can be approved quickly.
- **By-right infill:** in well-serviced established areas, compliant medium-density housing should not need a bespoke discretion.
- **Infrastructure-first precincts:** fund the transport, schools, water and open space before or alongside homes, not after.
- **One housing authority:** consolidate the state's housing delivery functions into a single accountable entity with a balance sheet and a pipeline.
- **Social housing as infrastructure:** a long-term asset class with stable revenue, not a annual grant program.
- **Renters' deal:** stable leases, minimum standards, a renters' portal and a fair process for rent increases.

## 5. Cut here

| Cut / stop / move | Current owner | What happens to the people | Saving or capacity released | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consolidate overlapping housing and development entities | DFFH/Homes Victoria/Development Victoria/VPA | Redeploy; protect delivery teams | Lower overhead, single pipeline | [D] |
| Replace bespoke approvals with pattern-book compliance in defined areas | DTP/councils | Planners move to precinct and compliance roles | Faster approvals, lower appeal load | [D] |
| Stop selling strategic public land for short-term receipts | DTF/Development Victoria | Land bank for housing and value capture | Long-term value, affordable homes | [D] |
| Simplify developer deeds and infrastructure contributions | DTP/councils | Fewer negotiations; standard terms | Lower transaction cost | [D] |
| Sunset low-value housing grants and duplicated programs | DFFH | Reallocate to pipeline and homelessness | Less admin churn | [D] |
| Replace stamp duty with a broad-based land tax over time | DTF/Treasurer | Transition support for affected households | Better mobility, stable revenue | [D] — politically contested |

**The hard calls:** Land value capture and planning reform will mobilise developers, councils and existing homeowners. The state must show that the value is shared, not captured by a few.
**What we will not cut:** Homelessness services; crisis accommodation; building safety; the rights of renters.

## 6. Add here

| Add / build / buy / guarantee | Why | Who delivers | Year 1 cost | Year 2+ cost | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public developer / land assembly vehicle | Assemble sites, fund infrastructure, capture value | Development Victoria/Homes Victoria | Capital | Revenue from sales and rents | Land receipts, value capture |
| Victorian pattern book and pre-approved designs | Faster, better, cheaper homes | DTP/VPA | Low | Low | Approval and construction savings |
| Infrastructure-first precinct program | Homes with services | DTP/Development Victoria/councils | Capital | Operating | Growth-area contributions |
| Social housing pipeline | Permanent affordable homes | Homes Victoria/CHPs | Capital | Operating subsidy | HAFF, state contribution, land |
| Renters' portal and standards | Transparency and quality | Consumer Affairs/CAV | Low | Low | Better compliance |
| Build-to-rent and institutional investment partnership | Long-term rental supply | Development Victoria/super funds | Facilitated | Market | Tax and planning settings |
| Housing data and delivery dashboard | Accountability | DTP/DFFH | Low | Low | Existing data |

**The new capability:** The state must be able to **assemble land, structure finance, manage a development pipeline, procure at scale and enforce quality**. It cannot do that through grants alone.

**The new deal:** The citizen gets a home they can afford in a place with services. The developer gets certainty. The council gets infrastructure. The state gets a permanent affordable housing asset and a share of the value.

## 7. The ecosystem deal

| Party | What they give | What they get | What happens if they do not move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commonwealth | HAFF, tax settings, migration planning | Housing supply and affordability | States absorb the shortfall |
| Local government | Up-to-date planning schemes, precinct delivery | Infrastructure funding, certainty, shared services | Housing targets missed; state intervenes |
| Developers / builders | Delivery at scale, quality, affordable share | Faster approvals, land, infrastructure | Supply stays short |
| Community housing providers | Tenancy management, community outcomes | Long-term pipeline and revenue | Social housing gap widens |
| Renters / households | Engagement and patience | Security, quality, affordability | Political backlash |
| Unions / workforce | Skills and productivity | Good jobs, training | Labour constraints slow delivery |
| Banks / super funds | Finance and long-term capital | Stable returns, policy certainty | Build-to-rent stalls |
| First Peoples | Land and cultural authority | Self-determination, housing outcomes | Culturally unsafe delivery |

**The convening mechanism:** A Housing Delivery Board chaired by the Premier or Treasurer, with Homes Victoria, DTP, VPA, Development Victoria, councils, CHPs and industry.
**The accountability mechanism:** A public housing pipeline dashboard: approvals, starts, completions, affordable share, infrastructure, waitlist and cost per home.

---

## 8. Cross-portfolio joins

- **Transport and roads** — housing without transport is displacement; precincts must be sequenced with transit.
- **Local government** — councils approve and deliver local infrastructure; rate caps and cost-shifting shape their capacity.
- **Regional Victoria** — housing is a workforce and population issue, not just a metro issue.
- **Skills, jobs and industry** — construction labour, trades and planning capability are the binding constraints.
- **Energy and climate** — new homes must be efficient, all-electric-ready and resilient.
- **Fiscal and public sector** — land, value capture and stamp duty reform are fiscal decisions.

**The shared asset:** A single land and housing pipeline that shows every site, approval, infrastructure need, tenure and completion date.

**The one number that matters most:** Net new homes completed per year, in the right places.
**The leading indicator:** The number of pre-approved, infrastructure-ready sites.
**The kill switch:** If value capture is not producing affordable homes, or if quality falls, redesign the model.

## 11. Open questions and sources

**What we do not know:**

- The true cost of approval delay and appeal risk per dwelling.
- The amount of surplus public land suitable for housing.
- The fiscal and distributional impact of replacing stamp duty with land tax.
- The delivery capacity of the community housing sector.
- The effect of the 2026 planning reforms on approval times and appeals.

**What we will commission or test in the first year:**

- A public land audit.
- A pattern-book pilot in three councils.
- A value-capture trial in one precinct.
- An independent review of housing pipeline costs.
