# 10 — Justice, Police and Community Safety

> **Citizen promise:** Safe communities, fair consequences, fewer victims.
>
> **Operator thesis:** Victoria keeps adding police, courts, prisons and youth justice capacity while the same people cycle through the system. The next move is to **separate safety from punishment**: invest in the front end (diversion, bail support, mental health, housing) and the back end (rehabilitation, resettlement), while keeping the community safe and the system accountable.
>
> **2026 baseline:** Bail laws were tightened from **31 March 2026**, and the **Youth Justice Act 2024** fully commenced on **30 September 2026**, raising the minimum age of criminal responsibility from 10 to 12. The government has committed about **$1.6 billion over four years** to youth justice capacity, while the Auditor-General reports alleged youth-offender incidents rose **40% between 2022 and 2025**. [A]

**Minister(s):** Minister for Police, Emergency Services, Multicultural Affairs and Crime Prevention (Paul Edbrooke); Attorney-General, Planning and Prevention of Family Violence (Sonya Kilkenny); Minister for Youth Justice, Corrections, Victims Support and Community Sport (Ros Spence).
**Department(s):** Department of Justice and Community Safety (DJCS).
**Key agencies:** Victoria Police; Courts Victoria; Corrections Victoria; Youth Justice; Office of Public Prosecutions; Victoria Legal Aid; IBAC (independent); Victorian Ombudsman; victim support services; community legal centres.
**Statute / framework:** Bail Act; Youth Justice Act; Sentencing Act; Corrections Act; Crimes Act; Family Violence Protection Act; Charter of Human Rights.
**Scale:** Police, courts, corrections and youth justice are among the largest state workforces and budgets. [B]

---

## 1. The outcome

- **Outcome 1:** Fewer people are victims of crime.
- **Outcome 2:** The justice system is fair, timely and accountable.
- **Outcome 3:** People who can be safely diverted are diverted; people who pose a risk are managed.
- **Outcome 4:** Prisons and youth justice reduce reoffending, not just detain people.
- **Outcome 5:** Victims are supported and their voice is central.

**What success looks like by 2030:** Lower repeat victimisation; fewer people on remand who could be safely supported in the community; reduced reoffending; faster court outcomes; and a measurable reduction in over-representation of First Peoples and people with mental illness or disability.

**What failure looks like:** More prisons, more remand, more police, but the same cycle and the same communities carrying the cost.

**What the citizen experiences:** A report to police, a long wait, a court date, a sentence, a release — often without information or closure. Victims feel sidelined. Offenders leave prison without housing, work or treatment.
**What the frontline experiences:** Court delays, remand pressure, mental health and housing gaps, administrative burden and a system that measures inputs.

---

## 3. Where the money and time leak

- **Remand growth:** Bail refusals and revocations rose more than 90% between 2024 and 2026, driving remand demand and prison costs. [A]
- **Court delays:** Backlogs and adjournments increase costs and harm victims and defendants.
- **Recidivism:** Prisons release people without housing, treatment, skills or work, so they return.
- **Mental health and disability:** People with unmet needs cycle through police, courts and custody.
- **Youth justice:** Custody is expensive and can entrench offending; community-based alternatives are under-resourced.
- **Victim support:** Fragmented services and limited information.
- **Data and accountability:** Agencies do not share a consistent view of the same person or outcome.
- **First Peoples over-representation:** A structural failure that requires self-determined solutions.

**Estimated annual value of the leak:** To be costed. The biggest items are remand, court delay, prison beds, recidivism and the cost of victimisation. [D]

## 4. The reframe

**The old frame:** Safety is delivered by police, courts and prisons; more capacity equals more safety.

**The new frame:** Safety is a **community outcome** produced by prevention, crisis response, diversion, rehabilitation and accountability working together.

**Three alternative models:**

| Model | How it works | Upside | Downside | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. More enforcement and custody** | More police, remand and prisons | Visible; public reassurance | Expensive; does not reduce reoffending | Necessary in part, not sufficient |
| **B. Front-end diversion and back-end resettlement** | Bail support, mental health, housing, court reform, rehabilitation | Lower reoffending and cost | Requires cross-agency coordination | Preferred base |
| **C. Community safety partnerships** | Local plans with police, councils, health, housing, community | Place-based prevention | Governance complexity | Strong complement |

**The reframe that matters most:** **Safety is more than police.** The state should fund the front end and the back end with the same seriousness as the prison pipeline, and measure the whole system by fewer victims and less reoffending.

**Supporting moves:**

- **Bail support and supervised accommodation:** safe alternatives to remand for people who do not pose a serious risk.
- **Court reform:** case management, virtual hearings where appropriate, and early resolution.
- **Corrections as rehabilitation:** education, work, health, housing and family connection from day one.
- **Youth justice reset:** community-based alternatives, diversion, restorative justice and custody as a last resort.
- **Victim support guarantee:** information, counselling and practical help.
- **Community safety plans:** local data and partnerships.
- **First Peoples justice:** self-determined diversion, healing and community-led solutions.

## 5. Cut here

| Cut / stop / move | Current owner | What happens to the people | Saving or capacity released | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consolidate duplicate corporate and administrative functions across justice agencies | DJCS | Shared services; redeploy | Overhead | [D] |
| Reduce avoidable remand through bail support | Courts/Corrections/community | Support in community | Prison and remand beds | [D] |
| Divert low-risk youth from custody | Youth Justice/community | Community-based programs | Custody cost | [D] |
| Sunset low-value justice programs without outcome evidence | DJCS | Reallocate to diversion and rehabilitation | Program savings | [D] |
| Reduce court adjournments through case management | Courts | Faster outcomes | Court time | [D] |

**The hard calls:** Bail and youth justice reform are politically contested, especially after high-profile crimes. The state must be able to say what it will do to keep the community safe, not just what it will stop.
**What we will not cut:** Police response; victim support; serious offender management; court safety.

## 6. Add here

| Add / build / buy / guarantee | Why | Who delivers | Year 1 cost | Year 2+ cost | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bail support and supervised accommodation | Safe alternatives to remand | DJCS/community | Moderate | Operating | Remand beds |
| Court case management and digital | Faster, fairer outcomes | Courts/DJCS | Capital | Operating | Court delay |
| Rehabilitation and resettlement | Reduce reoffending | Corrections/community | Moderate | Operating | Prison beds |
| Youth diversion and restorative justice | Better outcomes for young people | Youth Justice/community | Moderate | Operating | Custody |
| Victim support guarantee | Information and help | DJCS/victim services | Moderate | Operating | Trust and outcomes |
| Community safety plans | Local prevention | Councils/police/community | Low | Operating | Crisis costs |
| First Peoples justice solutions | Self-determination and healing | Aboriginal organisations | Moderate | Operating | Over-representation |

**The new capability:** The system must be able to **assess risk, support people in the community, share data and commission rehabilitation**.

**The new deal:** The community gets safety. The victim gets support. The offender gets a genuine path out. The state gets lower reoffending and a more sustainable system.

## 7. The ecosystem deal

| Party | What they give | What they get | What happens if they do not move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commonwealth | Family law, NDIS, federal offences | Better outcomes | State absorbs costs |
| Police | Data, diversion, community engagement | Trust and cooperation | Enforcement alone |
| Courts | Case management and timeliness | Resourcing and reform | Backlogs |
| Community organisations | Support, housing, treatment | Stable funding | Gaps and cycling |
| Health / mental health | Crisis and treatment | Less justice contact | Criminalisation |
| Housing | Accommodation | Stability | Recidivism |
| First Peoples | Cultural authority | Self-determination | Over-representation continues |
| Victims / community | Engagement | Safety and closure | Trust falls |

**The convening mechanism:** A Community Safety Board with DJCS, police, courts, corrections, youth justice, health, housing, councils and community.
**The accountability mechanism:** A public dashboard of crime, victimisation, remand, court delay, reoffending and over-representation.

---

## 8. Cross-portfolio joins

- **Mental health and wellbeing** — crisis alternatives and treatment reduce justice contact.
- **Families, children and community** — child protection, family violence and youth.
- **Housing and planning** — stable housing reduces reoffending.
- **First Peoples and multicultural affairs** — self-determination and justice.
- **Local government** — community safety planning.
- **Emergency management** — police and emergency response.
- **Digital, data and AI** — shared data and risk assessment.

**The shared asset:** A single, consent-based view of a person's justice, health, housing and service history.

| Dimension | Metric | Baseline | Target | Source | Frequency |

| Reoffending | Return to custody | To be baselined | Down | DJCS | Annual |
| Youth | Youth custody | To be baselined | Down | DJCS | Quarterly |
| Victims | Victim support access | To be baselined | Up | DJCS | Quarterly |
| Equity | First Peoples over-representation | To be baselined | Down | DJCS | Annual |
| Trust | Community confidence in justice | To be baselined | Up | Survey | Annual |

**The one number that matters most:** Repeat victimisation, because it measures whether the system is actually making people safer.
**The leading indicator:** The number of people on remand who could be safely supported in the community.
**The kill switch:** If diversion increases serious offending or victimisation, stop and redesign.

---

## 11. Open questions and sources

**What we do not know:**

- The share of remand that could be safely diverted.
- The cost and effectiveness of bail support.
- The drivers of youth offending and the best diversion models.
- The court delay causes by case type.
- The over-representation of First Peoples and people with disability.

**What we will commission or test in the first year:**

- A remand and bail support evaluation.
- A court delay audit.
- A youth justice diversion trial.
- A victim support review.
