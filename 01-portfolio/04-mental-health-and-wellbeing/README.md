# 04 — Mental Health and Wellbeing

> **Citizen promise:** Help before crisis, and no wrong door.
>
> **Operator thesis:** Victoria has built the institutions of mental health reform. It has not yet built the **front door**. The next move is to make community-led, voluntary care the default, with crisis alternatives that keep people out of emergency departments and police cells — and to fund housing, peer workforce and outcomes, not just beds.
>
> **2026 baseline:** The Mental Health and Wellbeing Commission assesses **18 of 74 recommendations in place, 52 partial and 4 not commenced**. **22 Mental Health and Wellbeing Locals** operate across 24 locations; public mental health staffing has grown from **8,397 FTE in 2021 to 11,205 FTE in 2025**. [A]

**Minister(s):** Minister for Health, Mental Health, Ambulance Services and Special Minister of State (Ingrid Stitt).
**Department(s):** Department of Health (DH).
**Key agencies:** Mental Health and Wellbeing Commission (MHWC); Mental Health and Wellbeing Locals; Victorian Collaborative Centre for Mental Health and Wellbeing; area mental health services; crisis assessment and treatment teams; community mental health organisations; Primary Health Networks (Commonwealth).
**Statute / framework:** Mental Health and Wellbeing Act 2022; Royal Commission into Victoria's Mental Health System recommendations; National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Agreement.
**Scale:** 22 Locals; 11,205 public mental health FTE; significant community and clinical service footprint. [A]

---

## 1. The outcome

- **Outcome 1:** People get help early, in their community, without needing a crisis or a diagnosis.
- **Outcome 2:** Crisis care is safe, humane and available outside ED and police.
- **Outcome 3:** People have a home, a purpose and a community, not just a treatment plan.
- **Outcome 4:** The workforce reflects lived experience and the community it serves.
- **Outcome 5:** Reform is measured by outcomes and experience, not announcements.

**What success looks like by 2030:** Fewer mental health presentations to ED; shorter waits for community care; more peer-led and community-led services; stable workforce; reduced seclusion and restraint; and measurable improvement in housing, employment and social connection.

**What failure looks like:** More services, more plans and more beds, but the same crisis-driven system and the same people falling through the gaps.

**What the citizen experiences:** A confusing set of phone numbers, eligibility rules and waiting lists. They may be told they are "not unwell enough" for one service and "too unwell" for another. In crisis, they may end up in ED or with police.
**What the frontline experiences:** High demand, workforce shortages, administrative burden, risk aversion, and a system that rewards acute response over prevention.

---

## 3. Where the money and time leak

- **Crisis concentration:** Too much spend goes to acute and crisis care because early and community care are hard to access.
- **ED and police demand:** People in distress present to ED or come to police attention because there is no safe alternative.
- **Housing gap:** Many people cannot leave hospital or stabilise because they have no safe home.
- **Workforce instability:** Recruitment and retention are difficult, especially in regional and specialised services.
- **Fragmented services:** Locals, clinical services, GPs, NGOs, housing, justice and schools do not share a plan or a record.
- **Compulsory treatment and seclusion:** Reform commitments are not fully implemented; practice varies.
- **Digital gaps:** Referrals, records and outcomes are not integrated.
- **Evaluation gap:** Many reforms are not yet evaluated against outcomes.

**Estimated annual value of the leak:** To be costed. The avoidable costs sit in ED, inpatient beds, police time, courts, prisons and homelessness services. [D]

## 4. The reframe

**The old frame:** Mental health is a clinical service you access when you are unwell enough.

**The new frame:** Mental health is a **community wellbeing system** with clinical care as one part. The front door is local, voluntary, peer-supported and connected to housing, employment, education and justice.

**Three alternative models:**

| Model | How it works | Upside | Downside | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. More clinical capacity** | More beds, more clinicians, more crisis teams | Meets acute demand | Expensive; does not shift the front door | Necessary but not sufficient |
| **B. Community-led front door** | Locals, peer workforce, crisis alternatives, housing-first | Early help; less crisis | Requires workforce and trust | Preferred base |
| **C. Integrated wellbeing system** | Shared budget across health, housing, justice, education, employment | Prevention and continuity | Complex governance | Long-term direction |

**The reframe that matters most:** **No wrong door.** A person should be able to walk into a Local, call one number, or be referred by a GP, school, police or housing service and get a single plan — regardless of eligibility.

**Supporting moves:**

- **Crisis alternatives:** safe spaces, peer-led crisis houses, residential step-up/step-down, and non-police response where safe.
- **Housing-first:** secure housing as a clinical intervention, with support attached.
- **Peer workforce:** paid, credentialed lived-experience roles across the system.
- **Outcome contracts:** fund community providers for housing, employment, social connection and experience, not just activity.
- **Digital front door:** one number, one record, one plan, with privacy and consent.
- **Aboriginal-led services:** culturally safe, self-determined care.

## 5. Cut here

| Cut / stop / move | Current owner | What happens to the people | Saving or capacity released | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce duplicated administrative and reporting layers across services | DH/health services | Shared services; redeploy | Admin time and cost | [D] |
| Shift spend from avoidable acute demand to community and crisis alternatives | DH/health services | Reinvest in Locals and alternatives | Bed days and ED demand | [D] |
| Sunset low-value programs without outcome evidence | DH | Reallocate to evaluated services | Program savings | [D] |
| Standardise referral, intake and assessment | DH/Locals/clinical services | One front door; less repetition | Admin and wait time | [D] |
| Reduce seclusion and restraint through practice change | Health services | Safer care | Quality and legal risk | [D] |

**The hard calls:** Shifting money from acute to community will be contested by hospitals and clinicians. It must be done with a safety guarantee and a transition plan.
**What we will not cut:** Crisis response; acute care for people who need it; Aboriginal-led services; peer support.

| Add / build / buy / guarantee | Why | Who delivers | Year 1 cost | Year 2+ cost | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expand Locals and community care | Early help, less crisis | DH/community providers | Moderate | Operating | Acute and ED demand |
| Crisis alternatives and safe spaces | Divert from ED and police | Community/health | Moderate | Operating | ED and justice costs |
| Housing-first partnerships | Stability and recovery | DH/DFFH/Homes Victoria | Capital and operating | Operating | Hospital and homelessness |
| Peer workforce program | Trust and engagement | DH/TAFEs/community | Low | Operating | Workforce capacity |
| Integrated digital front door | One number, one record | DH | Capital | Lower run cost | Duplicated systems |
| Outcome-based community contracts | Pay for results | DH/community | Low | Operating | Program churn |
| Aboriginal-led mental health services | Cultural safety and self-determination | Aboriginal community organisations | Moderate | Operating | Better outcomes |

**The new capability:** The system must be able to **commission community outcomes, integrate data, support peer workforce and manage risk outside hospital**.

**The new deal:** The citizen gets help early and a plan that follows them. The community provider gets stable funding. The hospital gets fewer avoidable presentations. The government gets measurable outcomes.

## 7. The ecosystem deal

| Party | What they give | What they get | What happens if they do not move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commonwealth | Medicare, NDIS, PHN integration | Better outcomes and lower acute costs | States absorb the gap |
| Health services | Clinical governance, data, referral pathways | Community demand management | ED remains the front door |
| Community providers | Local reach, peer workforce, trust | Multi-year outcome contracts | Fragmentation |
| Police / justice | Alternative response pathways | Less time in crisis and custody | Criminalisation continues |
| Housing / homelessness | Stable homes | Clinical stability | Bed block and relapse |
| Schools / employers | Early identification and support | Better participation | Demand shifts to crisis |
| People with lived experience | Expertise and co-design | Power and funding | Reform loses legitimacy |
| First Peoples | Cultural authority | Self-determination | Culturally unsafe care |

**The convening mechanism:** A Mental Health Reform Delivery Board with DH, MHWC, health services, community providers, housing, justice, education, police and lived-experience representatives.
**The accountability mechanism:** A public outcomes dashboard covering access, wait, crisis response, housing, seclusion, experience and suicide.

---

## 8. Cross-portfolio joins

- **Health and hospitals** — mental health is a core part of the flow system, not a separate silo.
- **Housing and planning** — housing is a clinical intervention.
- **Justice, police and community safety** — crisis alternatives and diversion reduce criminalisation.
- **Families, children and community** — early intervention and family support.
- **Education and early childhood** — school wellbeing and early identification.
- **Workforce and capability** — peer workforce and clinical pipeline.
- **Digital, data and AI** — one front door and shared records.

**The shared asset:** A single, consent-based care plan and record that follows the person across services.

| Quality | Seclusion and restraint | To be baselined | Down | MHWC | Quarterly |

| Experience | Consumer and carer experience | To be baselined | Up | MHWC | Annual |
| Outcomes | Suicide rate and repeated crisis | To be baselined | Down | ABS/DH | Annual |

**The one number that matters most:** The proportion of people who get help in the community before a crisis.
**The leading indicator:** The number of crisis alternatives and the time to access them.
**The kill switch:** If reform increases coercion or reduces access for the most unwell, stop and redesign.

---

## 11. Open questions and sources

**What we do not know:**

- The true demand and cost of crisis alternatives.
- The housing need among people using mental health services.
- The peer workforce pipeline and credentialing.
- The effect of Locals on ED presentations and admissions.
- The experience of Aboriginal and multicultural communities.

**What we will commission or test in the first year:**

- A crisis alternatives trial in three regions.
- A housing-first evaluation.
- A peer workforce census.
- A lived-experience survey.
